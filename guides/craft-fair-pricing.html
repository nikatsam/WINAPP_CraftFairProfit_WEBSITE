Product pricing · 5 minute read

How to think about handmade product prices.

A price needs to make sense for the product and the person making it. Start with the cost you can calculate, then test your assumptions against the whole event.

Reviewed 15 October 2026 · No universal markup or guaranteed selling price.

Know the unit cost behind the item

List the materials or purchase cost for one saleable unit. If you decide to account for your own making time, use a method that fits your business and avoid counting the same labour again in event expenses. Keep the assumption consistent so comparisons are useful.

Look at contribution, not just the margin label

For planning, the selling price less unit cost is the product’s contribution before any payment fee and event costs. A larger price-cost gap gives more room to cover the pitch and other event spending, but only if buyers choose that product at that price.

Example, for illustration only

A handmade item priced at £18 with a recorded unit cost of £6 has £12 of contribution before any payment fee and event-level expenses. That does not tell you whether £18 is suitable, whether the item will sell, or whether the event will make a profit.

Consider the wider plan

  • Include known material, packaging and production costs in a consistent unit-cost method.
  • Consider discounts and refunds when reviewing actual net sales.
  • Include the pitch and other fair-specific costs once, not inside every unit and again as fixed expenses.
  • Use the product mix and expected quantities when estimating an event, not only the best-selling item.
  • Compare a forecast to actual results after the fair, then adjust your assumptions.

Do not confuse a formula with market research

Costs can reveal the minimum contribution you need, but they cannot establish what a particular audience will pay. Research comparable products, materials, finish and your customers’ feedback. Keep any test small enough to manage, and change one assumption at a time where practical.
